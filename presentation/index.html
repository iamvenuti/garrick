Garrick

Leave any piece of work. Come back to the next action.

Garrick keeps track of where each piece of your work stands. It reads what comes in, keeps it up to date, and lets finished work step aside, so Claude picks up where you stopped.

Marco Venuti

Two things in one line. The headline is what Garrick does for you: you can leave any piece of work and come back to its next action. The subtitle is what it does with your context to make that return worth trusting. The rest of the deck takes them in that order.

The cost is in coming back

Interrupted work is picked up again, on average, 23 minutes and about two other tasks later.

And nearly one time in five, not the same day.

Gloria Mark, UC Irvine, observing 36 information workers over three working days. Gallup Business Journal, 2006.

State it exactly as measured. The 23 minutes is the time until people came back to the interrupted work, after about two other tasks. It says nothing about how long it takes to get going again, so don't stretch it. The one time in five is the one to dwell on: work that simply isn't picked up that day.

Concede what is true: with one line of work, the assistant's own memory is enough. The trouble starts with several pieces at once. A single ongoing stream, one conversation and one memory for everything, also works for one life with one confidence. It stops working the day your work has two sides, such as two clients, or a client and your employer. Name no product here; it is a direction, not a competitor.

The home lists every live piece of work, five at a time with the rest under more. A row whose next step is yours has a Continue button; a row waiting on someone has none. The Garrick app is an extra, set up by hand today; say so plainly. Everything on the home is read from the same one place per piece of work.
The whole idea in one picture. What you read and what was said are ingested into two graphs, the Knowledge and Meetings wikis, linked by topic and by who said what. With your context

The context life cycle

It takes context in, keeps it true, uses it within walls and lets it fade, so the place you come back to is still right.

They meet in one place per piece of work: leaving writes it, coming back reads it.

Everything so far was the first of the two. The second is why you can rely on it: a place to come back to is only useful if what it says is still true.

The context life cycle

One memory, walled use

What you're working on

your files

What was said

your meetings

What you read

your library

1 · Take in

Mail, transcripts and documents land in an inbox, and each is filed by who it came from, into your work, your meetings or your library.

2 · Keep true

The record says where each entry came from. "That's wrong" fixes every copy on your yes, and checks report what no longer matches.

3 · Use within walls

The right part of the record reaches the task. If your work holds other people's confidences, you can declare walls: the agent gets rules for what may not cross, and every commit of your work is checked against them.

4 · Let fade

Finished work steps aside with nothing in it lost. Old meetings and reading fade out of the way in stages, still findable.

Start with the top row: Garrick holds three things, what you're working on in your files, what was said in your meetings, and what you read in your library. A new install starts both wikis empty, and someone who never records a meeting may only ever have the first. One memory: every conversation is kept in one place, so you can find your own record. Walled use: the walls apply where material is used, not where it is stored. Walls are for people with a second party, such as two clients; with one employer and one confidence you never meet them. Be straight about their limits: the rule is one the assistant follows, and the check catches names, links and copied wording, while a clean paraphrase can pass. Fading covers old meetings and reading in the two wikis, never a piece of work, and nothing is deleted.

Where it lives

Yours, in plain files

Your folders stay as they are. Garrick reads them and writes nothing there unless you ask it to move the files in.

Its own record is plain Markdown in folders on your Mac, and updates keep your edits.

Claude does the work underneath. Codex works too, in its app or a terminal, without the home's Continue.

Gone in one step

Delete the folder. In a workspace of your own, one command puts the example away.

python3 examples/example-work/build.py --target ~/Garrick-example
In a guided session, run this line live. The example builds with dates relative to today, so the launch email really was left yesterday. Continue needs the Garrick app and the Claude Code command-line tool; without them, ask Claude "where am I on the Kestle chair launch?" in the new folder.

Yours to try

Move two pieces of work in. Come back to them through Garrick for a month.

Leave them when you need to, and come back with Continue or "where am I", not through chat history. Garrick was built from one person's working week; a month tells you whether it fits yours.
